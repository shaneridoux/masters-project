\section*{Abstract}  % Unnumbered section
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}  % Add to Table of Contents
\par
Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) is a complex autoimmune disease characterized by the erroneous destruction of pancreatic islet $\beta$-cells resulting in life-long dependence on exogenous insulin. Although T1D is considered to be largely genetic, with the heritability of the disease around 80\%, roughly 20\% of the phenotypic variance attributed to genetics is still unexplained. Epistasis, the interaction of genetic variants, is thought to contribute to this missing heritability in T1D, yet the epistatic landscape of T1D and its precursor Islet Autoimmunity (IA) remains understudied. A genome-wide analysis of the epistatic architecture of IA could inform potential biological pathways in the etiology of T1D in addition to elucidating aspects of its missing heritability. 
\par
After quality control, 359 DAISY children selected for nested case-control study with a customized Exome array imputed to the TOPMed r2 reference panel were included for analysis of genome-wide epistasis detection. Within-gene synergy networks were used to capture non-linear epistatic effects and summarized through diffusion kernel principal components (kPC). Between-gene epistasis was detected through a Bayesian interaction analysis of kPC-summarized genes.
\par
Posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) derived from the Bayesian model identified candidate genes and gene-pairs with potential involvement in IA onset. While convergence diagnostics indicated poor MCMC mixing, PIPs were still obtained for downstream prioritization. Pathway enrichment analysis of genes with high PIPs revealed strong overrepresentation in Reactome pathways related to the immune system, as well as tissue-specific downregulation in the pancreas. A power analysis constrained SNP-level follow-up testing to seven pairs, one of which--between \textit{PMM2} and \textit{NOD2}--approached nominal significance (odds ratio = 5.08; 95\% CI: [0.94, 27.56]).
\par
This study presents a genome-wide, multi-stage framework for detecting both within- and between-gene epistasis in genomic data. While limited by small sample size, the results reveal biologically coherent signals aligned with known mechanisms of T1D and IA, including innate immunity and MHC class II antigen-presentation. The use of diffusion-based gene summaries and Bayesian variable selection enabled interpretable prioritization of candidate loci and interactions despite the high dimensionality of the data. Findings from this study warrant further investigation in larger cohorts to validate emerging epistatic signals and explore their functional relevance in the etiology of IA and T1D.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{Type 1 Diabetes \& Islet Autoimmunity}
\label{sec:ia}
Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) is a complex autoimmune disorder where the immune system erroneously targets and destroys insulin-producing pancreatic islet-$\beta$ cells, leading to a lack of insulin and elevated blood glucose levels\cite{katsarouType1Diabetes2017}. Unlike \textit{monogenic} diseases -- such as sickle cell disease or cystic fibrosis, which result from variation within a single gene and are relatively rare -- \textit{complex} diseases are influenced by multiple chromosomal regions containing genetic variation, known as loci \cite{MonogenicDisorderOverview}. The natural history of T1D is believed to follow a continuous progression, beginning with the appearance of islet autoantibodies, followed by $\beta$-cell destruction, the onset of dysglycemia, and ultimately, the manifestation of hyperglycemia-related symptoms. Its pre-clinical stage, known as Islet Autoimmunity (IA), is characterized by the presence of autoantibodies prior to clinical diagnosis \cite{katsarouType1Diabetes2017,onengut-gumuscuGeneticVariantsComplement2024}. However, the precise onset of IA remains somewhat ambiguous. The definition used by the Diabetes AutoImmunity Study in the Young (DAISY\cite{rewersNewbornScreeningHLA1996a}) cohort classifies IA as the presence of one or more islet autoantibodies on two or more consecutive visits, or a single autoantibody-positive visit followed by a Type 1 Diabetes diagnosis at the next visit \cite{frohnertLateonsetIsletAutoimmunity2017}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{trialnet.png}
    \caption{{\bf Stages of T1D}\\
    The progression of T1D follows three recognized stages. \textbf{Stage 1} marks the onset of Islet Autoimmunity (IA), with the presence of two or more diabetes-related autoantibodies but normal blood glucose levels. \textbf{Stage 2} is characterized by worsening $\beta$-cell function and emerging dysglycemia, despite the absence of symptoms. \textbf{Stage 3} is the clinical onset of T1D, marked by symptomatic hyperglycemia requiring insulin therapy \cite{Type1Diabetes,onengut-gumuscuGeneticVariantsComplement2024}.}
    \label{fig:stages_t1d}
\end{figure}
\par
Often diagnosed in early childhood, T1D left untreated can result in serious complications such as diabetic ketoacidosis, a life-threatening condition. With the disease incidence rising \cite{atkinsonType1Diabetes2014}, efforts to understand the onset of IA and progression to T1D continue with the hopes of phase-specific therapeutic intervention to mitigate life-threatening complications, eliminate the burden of insulin pumps, and improve quality of life.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{rising-incidence-t1d.png}
    \caption{{\bf Incidence of T1D in children aged 0-14 years} \\
    The incidence of T1D has steadily increased across multiple countries over the past several decades, as shown in this longitudinal dataset\cite{atkinsonType1Diabetes2014}.}
    \label{fig:incidence_t1d}
\end{figure}
\par

T1D is largely a genetic disease, with environmental factors-—such as viral infections—-potentially influencing disease progression and the onset of IA. Ongoing research continues to investigate the viral onset hypothesis and the role of the complement system, which is primarily encoded within the Human Leukocyte Antigen (HLA) region \cite{onengut-gumuscuGeneticVariantsComplement2024}. However, the focus of this research remains on genetic susceptibility.
\par
The HLA region represents the strongest and longest-known genetic risk factor for T1D. The first association between HLA and juvenile-onset T1D was reported in the early 1970s through HLA typing of blood serum\cite{singalHistocompatibilityHLAAntigens1973}. This finding was later validated in the 1980s using targeted genetic association testing, which pinpointed specific HLA-DQB1 alleles as key determinants of T1D susceptibility \cite{toddHLADQBGeneContributes1987}. This highly polymorphic region has been associated with many other autoimmune diseases and constitutes roughly 40\% of T1D \textit{heritability}\cite{onengut-gumuscuGeneticVariantsComplement2024}--that is, 40\% of the variance in T1D status within the population that can be attributed to genetic factors.
\par
Despite decades of research, questions about the genetic architecture of T1D remain. Over 60 candidate loci, collectively explaining roughly 80\% of the heritability\cite{pangMissingHeritabilityType2022}, have been identified with respect to T1D. However, this leaves approximately 20\% of heritability unexplained, a phenomenon known as the missing heritability problem. Addressing this gap requires further investigation into rare variants, common variants with small effect sizes, and gene-gene interactions (epistasis)\cite{pangMissingHeritabilityType2022,roseEpistasisImmunityRole2012}.

\subsection{Epistasis}
\label{sec:epi}
\textit{Epistasis} has been defined and redefined many times since its coinage by Bateson in 1909, often leading to ambiguity in its definition. Seldom mentioned, however, is that Bateson also defined the term \textit{hypostasis} \cite{batesonMendelsPrinciplesHeredity1909,cordellEpistasisWhatIt2002}. These two terms were initially meant to describe a hierarchical relationship where a higher-level factor (epistatic gene/allele) controls whether a lower-level factor (hypostatic gene/allele) can be expressed. Here, \textit{epistatic} and \textit{hypostatic} derive from the Greek prefixes \textit{epi} meaning "upon", \textit{hypo} meaning "under", and root \textit{stasis} meaning "standing". Thus, to Bateson, an epistatic gene "stands upon" a hypostatic gene, masking the hypostatic gene's expression. 
\par
Epistasis, or \textit{dual epistacy}, was redefined under a statistical context by Fisher in 1918 as a deviation from linearity in allelic effects with respect to some quantitative phenotype $D$ \cite{cordellEpistasisWhatIt2002,fisherXVTheCorrelationRelatives1919}. Here, we already see differing definitions of epistasis emerging just 9 years apart, each carrying distinct implications. While Fisherian epistasis does not necessarily imply biological or functional epistasis, it provides a framework for detecting genetic interactions that warrant further investigation. The modern definition of epistasis is largely an umbrella term for genetic interaction. Under this umbrella, we find the term \textit{positive/negative epistasis}, which refers to the interaction between genetic variants where positive (negative) epistasis occurs when their combined effect on a trait or disease risk is greater (less) than the sum of their individual effects. At the population level, this deviation from additivity plays a crucial role in shaping genetic architecture and disease susceptibility \cite{johnsonEpistasisEvolutionRecent2023}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{epistasis.jpg}
    \caption{{\bf Gene-Gene Interaction} \\
    This is a conceptual model showing how Gene-Gene interactions play a role in phenotypic variation through multiple molecular interactions, including DNA-DNA, protein-DNA, protein-RNA, and protein-protein interactions \cite{wangMeaningInteraction2010}.}
    \label{fig:epistasis}
\end{figure}
\par 
Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) are a modern approach to discover genetic associations with a disease. GWAS test millions of representative single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from a population in a linear framework where each SNP is tested for association with a phenotype individually. The standard correction to account for multiple hypothesis testing in GWAS is the Bonferroni correction, which adjusts for the number of independent SNPs in the genome. This method typically applies a genome-wide significance threshold of $5\times 10^{-8}$ for European populations to identify statistically significant associations and similar but more stringent threshold for African populations \cite{evangelouGeneticEpidemiologyMethods2018,shamStatisticalPowerSignificance2014}.
\par
There are issues with this standard of practice. The standard genome-wide significance threshold faces challenges in evolving GWAS practices. It was developed for common variants, potentially lacking power for rare variants. It does not take into account the prior probability of a variant being associated with a phenotype, nor does it account for the statistical power of the test which both influence the interpretation of the $p$-value. These oversights can distort results, particularly when considering epistasis, where interactions between variants may not align with assumptions of independence and testing SNPs in pairs or tuples of length $n$ drastically increase the already large number of hypotheses. Applying the standard threshold universally risks missing meaningful associations while inflating false positives, especially in diverse populations or complex phenotypes\cite{jiangBayesianMethodEvaluating2011,shamStatisticalPowerSignificance2014}.
\par
Genome-wide association interaction (GWAI) studies are an extension of GWAS aimed at epistasis detection\cite{bessonovCautionaryNoteImpact2015,gusarevaPracticalAspectsGenomewide2014}. There are two categories of methods that emerge when considering GWAI: exhaustive and non-exhaustive\cite{gusarevaPracticalAspectsGenomewide2014}. Non-exhaustive methods avoid the challenge of multiple testing correction by limiting the analysis in some way. Typically, these analyses determine candidate genes a priori, use GWAS results to look at interactions between significant SNPs overlooking those SNPs with small effects with potentially large interaction effects, or use some sort of dimensionality reduction\cite{gusarevaPracticalAspectsGenomewide2014,anChallengeDetectingEpistasis2009}. To address the burden of multiple testing in exhaustive approaches, Bayesian methods have been recommended for both GWAS and GWAI\cite{jiangBayesianMethodEvaluating2011,stephensBayesianStatisticalMethods2009,lopezBayesianStatisticalMethods,zhouPolygenicModelingBayesian2013,shamStatisticalPowerSignificance2014}.

\subsection{Bayesian Statistics}
\label{sec:bayes}
Bayesian statistics is named after Reverend Thomas Bayes, a part-time mathematician who was among the first recorded to attempt solving the problem of inverse probability\cite{shoemakerBayesianStatisticsGenetics1999,gelmanBayesianDataAnalysisa}. Inverse probability seeks to determine the probability of a cause given an observed outcome as opposed to classical probability which estimates the likelihood of an event given known conditions. In Bayes' work, that was posthumously communicated by his friend and colleague Richard Price, he posed the problem 
\begin{quote}
    "\textit{Given} the number of times in which an unknown event has happened and failed: 
    \textit{Required} the chance that the probability of its happening in a single trial lies 
    somewhere between any two degrees of probability that can be named." \cite{bayesLIIEssaySolving1997}
\end{quote}

\noindent
Bayes' original work did not explicitly introduce what we now know as Bayes' theorem:
$$p(\theta \mid y) = \frac{p(y \mid \theta)p(\theta)}{p(y)}$$
This was instead formulated by Pierre-Simon Laplace. Bayes' contribution was rather the concept of compromising prior information, $p(\theta)$, with information from observed data, $p(y \mid \theta)$. He introduces this concept with an analogy to a billiard table where he considers a scenario where a ball is randomly thrown onto a unit interval, and the goal is to estimate the probability of a future event occuring based on where the ball landed. Bayes introduced what we would now call a uniform prior distribution on $\theta$ such that $\theta$ was modeled as the probability parameter of a binomial distribution\cite{bayesLIIEssaySolving1997,fienbergWhenDidBayesian2006}. 
\par
In population genetics, the classical frequentist approach to discovering associations with a phenotype typically involves computing the $p$-value for the null hypothesis ($H_0$) of no association. Despite our desire to interpret the $p$-value as the probability of $H_0$ being true (i.e. $p$-value $< 0.05$ implying significance through rejecting the null in favor of the alternative hypothesis $H_1$), this is simply not the case. The $p$-value relies not only on the how unlikely it is under $H_0$ but also how unlikely it is under $H_1$ which differs from study to study due to differences in power and measures the extremeness of the result under the null\cite{lesaffreBayesianBiostatistics2012}. A fundamental limitation of the $p$-value aside from its comparability across studies is that with-in a study, two genetic associations with equivalent $p$-values may have different levels of true association due to underlying factors such as minor allele frequency (MAF) which is the frequency of the non-dominant allele in the reference population. Some of these limitations can be addressed using the Bayesian framework, although doing so comes at the cost of increased modeling assumptions and often times computation complexity\cite{stephensBayesianStatisticalMethods2009}. 
\par
Modern Bayesian approaches incorporate prior probabilities and probabilistic modeling to improve statistical power and control false positives. In the context of a frequentist GWAS, the number of comparisons needs to be corrected for. The Bayesian perspective circumvents this requirement as the $p$-value analogue is the \textit{posterior probability}, $P(H_0 = \text{TRUE} \mid \text{Data})$, and is truly interpreted as a probability of true association conditioned on the observed data\cite{lesaffreBayesianBiostatistics2012}.

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:motiv}
Epistasis is particularly important in complex autoimmune diseases, where interactions between immune-related loci can shape immune system function and the loss of self-tolerance\cite{roseEpistasisImmunityRole2012}. T1D is a prime example of this.
While significant progress has been made in identifying genetic risk loci, including the well-established role of the HLA region, a substantial proportion of heritability remains unexplained\cite{onengut-gumuscuGeneticVariantsComplement2024}. This missing heritability may be partially attributed to epistasis, which is not captured by standard GWAS\cite{pangMissingHeritabilityType2022}.
\par
Traditional GWAS frameworks are optimized for detecting marginal effects of individual SNPs but are not well-suited for modeling the joint effects of interacting loci. When considering epistasis, the number of SNP pairs (or $n$-tuples) to test increases combinatorially, exacerbating the multiple testing problem and further reducing power. Furthermore, $p$-values in the frequentist framework are often misinterpreted, and their interpretation can vary substantially depending on study-specific factors \cite{lesaffreBayesianBiostatistics2012,walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}.
\par
Bayesian methods offer a compelling alternative. They are not only well-suited for discovery in large-scale genomic studies but also allow for probabilistic modeling of uncertainty and incorporation of prior knowledge. Unlike $p$-values, posterior probabilities are directly interpretable and provide a more intuitive framework for assessing evidence of association. In the context of epistasis, the Bayesian approach provides a natural way to mitigate the multiple testing problem while identifying subtle, biologically meaningful interactions that may contribute to disease risk\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}.
\par
GWAI studies have been relatively understudied in T1D and the methods for epistasis detection continue to evolve along with computational ability. We extend the work of \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022} and apply this extended method to IA, with the goal of elucidating the epistatic landscape that may shape the early progression of T1D.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:meth}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:meth-over}

To uncover the complex genetic interactions underlying IA in DAISY children, we altered the multi-stage analytic framework integrating information theory, graph-based modeling, and Bayesian nonlinear interaction analysis from \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}. Within-gene epistasis is quantified using an information-theoretic synergy score, capturing non-additive effects between SNP pairs. We then summarize each gene’s internal epistatic landscape by constructing a synergy matrix and applying diffusion kernel principal component analysis (kPCA) to derive new gene-level features.
\par
These non-linear summaries serve as inputs to a hierarchical Bayesian interaction model that was restricted to intra-chromosomal gene pairs, allowing us to flexibly capture gene-gene interactions while controlling for lower-order effects in a computationally feasible way. To further refine our focus, we identify high-priority genes and gene pairs based on their membership in top-ranked pathways from an Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) conducted using FUMA\cite{watanabeFunctionalMappingAnnotation2017}. We then revisit the SNP level within these prioritized regions and construct interaction networks to highlight specific variants that may drive gene-level epistatic signals relevant to IA.

\subsection{Data Preparation}
\label{sec:dataprep}
\subsubsection{DAISY}
The Diabetes Autoimmunity Study in the Young (DAISY) is a longitudinal birth cohort established in Denver, Colorado to investigate the genetic and environmental determinants of IA and progression to T1D in children. Children with high- or moderate-risk HLA genotypes were enrolled and followed prospectively for the development of islet autoantibodies and diabetes\cite{rewersNewbornScreeningHLA1996a}.

\subsubsection{Quality Control}
Quality control (QC) and data preprocessing were performed in three stages across various institutions: initial genotype QC and sex-check filtering were completed by a group at the University of Virgina; imputation to the TOPMed reference panel was conducted by Sarah D. Slack; and final QC was performed specifically for the study of epistasis which involves a more strict LD pruning threshold\cite{bessonovCautionaryNoteImpact2015}.
\par
Genotype data were initially available for 745 DAISY children with 622,925 SNPs. SNPs with low call rates (<80\%), monomorphic sites, and samples with missingness >5\% were removed, resulting in 744 individuals and 446,557 high-quality SNPs. Additional filters addressed sex discrepancies and sex chromosome anomalies, removing mislabeled or ambiguous samples and problematic X/Y chromosome variants, yielding 741 individuals and 445,541 SNPs. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium filtering ($P < 1\times 10^{-6}$ and $P < 1\times 10^{-20}$ for the MHC region) was applied, followed by imputation to the TOPMed r2 reference panel, expanding the variant set to 9,773,672 SNPs.
\par
The dataset was then subset to 409 DAISY participants with IA phenotype data available. Further sample level QC steps removed related individuals (in KING\cite{manichaikulRobustRelationshipInference2010,SecondgenerationPLINKRising} with a \textit{-king-cutoff} $= 0.177$), individuals with abnormal heterozygosity ($|F|>0.1$), and ethnic outliers identified via PCA. Strict linkage disequilibrium (LD) pruning (\textit{--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2} and \textit{--indep-pairwise 50 5 0.5} for \textit{CSMD1} due to its size) was applied in PLINK2\cite{SecondgenerationPLINKRising} to remove redundancy, retaining 1,627,974 variants. Finally, SNPs were annotated to the gene level using ANNOVAR\cite{ANNOVARFunctionalAnnotation}, and only those with valid mappings to the hg19 reference genome were kept. This resulted in a final dataset of 359 individuals, 685,455 annotated SNPs, and 20859 genes for downstream analysis.

\subsection{Summarizing Within-Gene Epistasis}
\label{sec:sum-within}
\subsubsection{Overview}
To model complex \textit{within-gene} SNP interactions that contribute to IA in DAISY children, we build from foundational concepts in information theory, such as entropy and mutual information, to quantify the epistatic contribution of SNP combinations. Using these quantities, we define pairwise synergy between SNPs and construct gene-specific synergy matrices reflecting potential non-linear effects. We then leverage the graph-theoretic properties associated with the Laplacian to model information flow across SNP-level interaction networks using diffusion kernels. Finally, by applying diffusion kernel principal component analysis (kPCA), we summarize each gene’s within-gene epistatic architecture as a single, non-linear gene-level feature for downstream between-gene epistasis analysis.

\subsubsection{Entropy}
\label{sec:entropy}
Claude Shannon, the father of information theory\cite{saraivaShannonEntropyIts2023}, introduced the concept of \textit{entropy} in \textit{A Mathematical Theory of Communication}\cite{shannonMathematicalTheoryCommunication} in 1948. Shannon proposed a measure of uncertainty $H(p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n)$ for probabilities $p_i$ such that $i \in \{1,2,\ldots,n\}$ with the following desirable properties:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $H$ should be continuous in the $p_i$. 
\item If all the $p_i$ are equal, $p_i = \frac{1}{n}$, then $H$ should be a monotonic increasing function of $n$. With equally likely events there is more choice, or uncertainty, when there are more possible events. $$H(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}) < H(\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}) < \cdots < H(\frac{1}{n},\ldots, \frac{1}{n})$$
\item If a choice be broken down into two successive choices, the original $H$ should be the weighted sum of the individual values of $H$. $$H(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{6}) = H(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}) + \frac{1}{2}H(\frac{2}{3},\frac{1}{3})$$

\end{enumerate}

Shannon proved (in Appendix 2 of \textit{A Mathematical Theory of Communication}\cite{shannonMathematicalTheoryCommunication}) that the only $H$ satisfying these three properties is:
$$H = -K\sum_{i=1}^np_i\log_b(p_i)$$
where $b$ is the base of the logarithm determining the units ($b=2 \implies bits$ and $b=e \implies nats$) and $K$ is a positive constant equivalent to the choice of a unit of measure (i.e. if you choose $b=2$ your units would be in \textit{bits} but if you also choose $K = \frac{1}{\log_2(e)}$ you can convert it \textit{nats}). For convenience, we will assume $b=2$ and $K=1$ and use the equivalent statement 
\begin{equation}
H = \sum_{i=1}^np_i\log(\frac{1}{p_i})
\label{eq:entropy}
\end{equation}
for the remainder of the paper. 
\par
While Shannon's derivation is a rigorous result of three desirable properties, a more intuitive approach to constructing $H$ starts with \textit{surprise}\cite{ngProbabilitySurprisesEntropy2022,saraivaShannonEntropyIts2023}.
\begin{proof}[Intuitive Justification]
Let $A$ be an event--for example winning the lottery. Let the probability of winning be $P(A)=p$. If $p$ is small, the surprise associated with $A$, denoted $S(A)$, should be large. Let's assume $S(A) = \frac{1}{p}$ as it captures this idea and alter it as needed. \\
If $p=1$, then $S(A) = 1$ but it would make more sense for the surprise to be $0$. Now let $S(A) = \log(\frac{1}{p})$. \\
If $p=1$, then $S(A) = 0$. As $p\to 0$, $S(A) \to \infty$. This is exactly what we would want as a surprise function. Since we know $P(A) = p$ is the probability of winning the lottery, the probability of losing the lottery is $P(A^c) = 1-P(A) = 1-p$. The expected surprise associated with winning the lottery would then be 
$$E[S(A)] = P(A)\cdot S(A) + P(A^c) \cdot S(A^c) = p\cdot \log(\frac{1}{p}) + (1-p)\cdot \log(\frac{1}{1-p})$$.
If we let $p_1 = p$ and $p_2 = 1-p$ then we can write: 
$$E[S(A)] = \sum\limits_{i=1}^2 p_i \log(\frac{1}{p_i}) = H.$$
Thus, entropy can be interpreted as the average surprise or the expected uncertainty of an outcome.
\end{proof}
The figure below illustrates the entropy $H(p)$, or expected uncertainty, as a function of the probability $p$ of $A$. Note the maximum entropy occurs when $p=0.5$. As $p\to 0$ and $p\to 1$ the event is more predictable and thus the entropy approaches 0.

\par
A natural extension is to consider the joint entropy of multiple events $X \in \mathcal{X}$, $Y\in \mathcal{Y}$ where $p(X,Y)$ is the joint probability. The entropy is then 
\begin{equation}
H(X,Y) = \sum\limits_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}p(X,Y)\log(\frac{1}{p(X,Y)})
\label{eq:joint_entropy}
\end{equation}
where $H(X,Y) \le H(X) + H(Y)$ with equality occurring if the event $X$ and $Y$ are independent such that $p(X,Y)=p(X)p(Y)\cite{shannonMathematicalTheoryCommunication}$. Another natural extension is then to define the conditional entropy of $X$ given $Y$: 
\begin{equation}
H(X\mid Y) = \sum_{\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}}p(X,Y)\log(\frac{1}{p(X\mid Y)}) = H(X,Y) - H(X)
\label{eq:cond_entropy}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Mutual Information}
\label{sec:mutual}
We now have a measure of the expected uncertainty associated with an event--namely, entropy--as well as a means of quantifying uncertainty in the presence of a dependent event, via conditional entropy. Figure~\ref{fig:entropy_relationship} highlights the overlap in information being communicated from both events when they depend on one another. We can quantify this redundancy as \textit{mutual information}, denoted:
$$I(X;Y) \equiv H(X) - H(X\mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid X) \equiv I(Y;X).$$
Note that $I(X;Y) \ge 0$ as $H(X\mid Y) \le H(X)$. This measures the average reduction in uncertainty about $X$ that results from having information about $Y$ or can equivalently be thought of as the average information gain when knowing information about both events as highlighted in Figure~\ref{fig:mutinfo_relationship}\cite{mackayInformationTheoryInference,walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022,huCharacterizingGeneticInteractions2011}.

\par
Importantly, the mutual information extends to joint variables. Let $X= (A,B)$. Then,
$$I(X;Y) = I(A,B;Y) = H(A,B) - H(A,B \mid Y) = H(Y) - H(Y\mid A,B) = I(Y;A,B) = I(Y;X).$$
The formulations of mutual information used throughout the remainder of this paper are:
\begin{equation}
I(A;D) = H(D) - H(D \mid A)
\label{eq:inf}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
I(A,B;D) = H(D) - H(D \mid A,B)
\label{eq:bi_inf}
\end{equation}
where $D$ denotes the \textit{disease} outcome and $A$ and $B$ represent SNPs. Thus, the interpretation of $I(A,B;D)$ is the mutual information of the disease $D$ and jointly knowing genotypes for SNP $A$ and SNP $B$. This is equivalent then to the difference in the expected uncertainty in the disease $D$ and jointly knowing genotypes for SNP $A$ and SNP $B$. In other words, $I(A,B;D)$ reflects the reduction in uncertainty about the disease status provided by jointly knowing the genotypes for $A$ and $B$\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}.

\subsubsection{Computing Within-Gene SNP Synergies}
\label{sec:withingene}
The concept of bivariate \textit{synergism} quantifies the combined effect of SNPs $S_i$ and $S_j$ on a disease $D$ beyond their individual contributions. It is calculated as:
\begin{equation}
Syn_{ij} \equiv Syn(S_i, S_j; D) = I(S_i,  S_j; D) -[I(S_i; D) + I(S_j; D)]
\label{eq:bivariate}
\end{equation}
A positive synergy, $Syn_{ij}>0$, implies that the average reduction in the uncertainty about the disease from \textit{jointly} knowing SNPs $S_i$ and $S_j$ is greater than the sum of their individual contributions. This indicates a deviation from linearity in their effects, suggesting a \textit{positive epistatic} interaction influencing disease status.
\par
Synergy can be extended to include not just pairwise interactions, but higher-order interactions. The general form for synergy with disease $D$ and SNPs $S_1, S_2 \ldots, S_n$ for $n\in \mathbb{N}$ where $\mathcal{S} = \{S_1, \ldots, S_n\}$ is:
\begin{equation}
\text{Syn}(S_1, S_2, \ldots, S_n; D) = \sum_{k=1}^{n} (-1)^{k} \sum_{\substack{s \subseteq \mathcal{S} \\ |s| = k}} I(s; D)
\label{eq:gen_syn}
\end{equation}
This formulation captures the deviation from additivity in the mutual information between the disease outcome and combinations of SNPs, accounting for double counting through the principle of inclusion and exclusion. However, the remainder of this paper will focus on bivariate synergy, where $n=2$ as seen in Equation~\ref{eq:bivariate}. 
\par
Using genotypes of SNPs annotated to the gene-level, we can construct pairwise synergies over all SNPs within each gene and define a \textit{synergy matrix} $\mathbf{S}$, with $diag(\mathbf{S}) = 0$, where each off diagonal entry $\mathbf{S}_{ij}$ corresponds to the pairwise synergy between SNPs $i$ and $j$ for $i\ne j$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix}
0 & \text{Syn}_{12} & \cdots & \text{Syn}_{1n}\\
\text{Syn}_{21} & 0 & \cdots & \text{Syn}_{2n}\\
\vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \vdots\\
\text{Syn}_{n1} & \text{Syn}_{n2} & \cdots & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
\label{eq:S}
\end{equation}
This synergy matrix enables the construction of SNP-level graphs within each gene, with interpretable edge weights reflecting potential pairwise epistatic interactions. To summarize the total pairwise epistatic contribution of each SNP, we define a diagonal matrix $\mathbf{D}$ whose entries are the row sums of $\mathbf{S}$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{D} = \begin{pmatrix}
\sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{S}_{1j} & 0 \cdots & 0 \\
0 & \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{S}_{2j} & \cdots & 0\\
\vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
0 & 0 & 0 & \sum_{j=1}^n \mathbf{S}_{nj}
\end{pmatrix}
\label{eq:D}
\end{equation}
We now define a \textit{Laplacian-esque} matrix $\mathbf{L}$ for each gene's synergy graph $G$ that captures both the pairwise epistasis and the aggregate pairwise epistasis: 
$$\mathbf{L}_{ij} =
\begin{cases}
-\text{Syn}_{ij} & \text{if } i \ne j, \\
\sum_{l=1}^n\text{Syn}_{il} & \text{if } i = j.
\end{cases}
$$ or alternatively
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{D} - \mathbf{S}
\label{eq:L}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Diffusion Kernel Principal Compents as Gene Summaries}
\label{sec:dkpc}
The Laplacian matrix has an intimate connection the \textit{heat equation} presented in physics that describes how heat and other diffusing quantities spread through a medium. Diffusion kernels are often referred to as "heat" kernels due to it being the fundamental solution to the \textit{heat equation}\cite{chungSpectralGraphTheory1997,kondorDiffusionKernelsGraphs}:
$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = -\mathcal{L}u.$$
Applied to graphs, diffusion can be thought of as the spread of information from node to node. Imagine a graph or network where the edges and nodes are made of metal. If we apply enough heat to one node so that we can visibly see the metal turn red, we can watch as the heat transfers from the node to which it was applied to its neighbors. This captures the spread of information to its direct neighbors as well as its indirect neighbors as depicted in Figure~\ref{fig:diffusion}. The \textit{kernel} summarizes this diffusion of information between nodes\cite{liOutofDistributionNodeDetection2024}.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics{Masters-Thesis-diffusion}
\caption{{\bf Diffusion Intuition}\\
The graph on the {\bf left} depicts nodes 1 and 7 as "hot", while the remaining nodes are "cold". After diffusion (graph on the {\bf right}), node 1 has "cooled" as it transfers heat to its direct neighbors—and to a lesser extent, its indirect neighbors—demonstrating the spread of information. In contrast, node 7 remains "hot" due to its lack of connections.}
    \label{fig:diffusion}
\end{figure}

\par
To summarize each gene's epistatic landscape while capturing the non-linearity present, we apply \textit{diffusion kernel principal companent analysis} (diffusion kPCA) to $\mathbf{L}$. The \textit{diffusion kernel} is defined as:
\begin{equation}
K_{L0} = e^{-\beta \mathbf{L}}
\label{eq:KL0}
\end{equation}
where $\beta\ge 0$ is a tuning parameter that controls the extent of diffusion or how easily information is able to be passed. As $\beta$ increases, it is easier for information to spread between nodes. A $\beta$ of 0 would imply no information is spread. Since the optimal value of $\beta$ for modeling epistasis is unknown, we followed the approach in \textit{Walakira et al.} \cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}, allowing $\beta$ to range from 0 to 10 in increments of 0.1 and averaging the resulting kernels to obtain the final kernel matrix:
\begin{equation}
K_L = \frac{1}{101}\sum_{i=1}^{101} K_{L0i}
\label{eq:KL}
\end{equation}

$K_L$ then summarizes the overall diffusion behavior, capturing how information spreads through the graph across a range of diffusion intensities, and in doing so, reflects the aggregate, pairwise, and higher-order epistatic architecture. 
\par
We can now apply this architecture to the genotype data to construct a kernel matrix $K$, which captures gene-level similarity between individuals based on each gene's epistatic profile.
\begin{equation}
K = GK_LG^T
\label{eq:K}
\end{equation}
where $G$ is an $n\times p$ genotype matrix for gene $g$, with $n$ individuals and $p$ SNPs. The resulting kernel matrix $K$ is then centered and subjected to eigendecomposition using the RSpectra package\cite{qiuRSpectraSolversLargescale2024}. The first principal component is then extracted and used as gene $g$'s within-gene epistasis summary.


\subsection{Between-Gene Epistasis Detection}
\label{sec:between-geen}
To model complex \textit{between-gene} interactions that contribute to IA in DAISY children, we restrict our analysis to intra-chromosomal gene-pairs. The motivation behind this is purely computational and chromosomes serve as a natural biological partition. Determining all combinations of gene-pairs genome-wide was intractable. While there is literature to support searching for inter-chromosomal interactions, this analysis will focus on intra-chromosomal gene-gene interactions.
\par
We transition from information-theoretic approaches to a \textit{Bayesian semi-parametric}\cite{antonelliEstimatingHealthEffects2019,walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022} framework, implemented in the NLinteraction\cite{antonelliEstimatingHealthEffects2019} R package. This enables flexible modeling of non-linear intra-chromosomal gene-gene interactions while maintaining interpretability and mitigating the burden of hypothesis correction during discovery. After identifying gene pairs with evidence of interaction, we reintroduce SNP-level data into the Synergy matrix $\mathbf{S}$, from Section~\ref{sec:withingene}, to pinpoint specific variants driving both cross-gene and within-gene epistatic effects contributing to IA.

\subsubsection{Bayesian Interaction Modeling}
\label{sec:bim}
For a pair of genes $\{g_1, g_2\} \subset \mathcal{G}$, where $\mathcal{G}$ is the set of all genes being analyzed, and for individual $i$, we take the first principal components from the within-gene epistasis summaries described in Section~\ref{sec:dkpc} and denote them individually as $\{X_{ig_1}, X_{ig_2}\}$ and their interaction as $X_{ig_1g_2}$. Interacting gene-pairs were determined to have evidence of epistasis based on a positive \textit{posterior inclusion probability} (PIP) which quantifies the probability that a given feature--or pair of features--is included in the model conditioned on the observed data. 
\par
To flexibly model smooth, non-linear relationships (see Figure~\ref{fig:splines}), we applied the natural spline basis function $ns(\cdot)$, implemented in R\cite{rcoreteamLanguageEnvironmentStatistical2023}, to each main effect $X_{g}$ for all $g \in \mathcal{G}$, as well as to each interaction term $X_{g_1g_2}$ for all $\{g_1, g_2\} \subset \mathcal{G}$. We denote the $ns(\cdot)$-transformed summaries as:
\begin{align}
\tilde{X}_{g_1} &= ns_1(X_{g_1}) \\
\tilde{X}_{g_2} &= ns_2(X_{g_2}) \\
\tilde{X}_{g_1g_2} &= ns_1(X_{g_1}) \cdot ns_2(X_{g_2})
\end{align}

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics{Masters-Thesis-splines}
\caption{{\bf Fitting with Natural Splines} \\
An illustration of natural spline smoothing applied to noisy, non-linear data. The gray points represent raw observations, while the blue curve shows the flexible fit obtained using natural spline basis functions. This demonstrates how splines can capture complex, multi-modal relationships without requiring a parametric form. As the degrees of freedom (df) in $ns(\cdot)$ increase, the fitted line becomes more flexible and approaches interpolation of the data, at the cost of smoothness.}
\label{fig:splines}
\end{figure}
The Bayesian model formulation makes the assumption of a normally distributed response. However, the binary phenotype (IA vs. non-IA) was residualized using the first seven ancestry principal components, following the recommendation of \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}. While this approach does not strictly satisfy the model assumptions, prior work\cite{pirinenEfficientComputationLinear2013} supports it as a computationally efficient approximation. Since our goal is not to perform direct inference, but rather to prioritize or rank genes and gene pairs for further investigation, this approximation is considered sufficient for discovery-oriented analysis.
\par
Let $Y$ be the ancestry corrected phenotype, $p$ be the number of genes, i.e., $|\mathcal{G}| = p$, and let $k$ be a number sufficiently large such that all gene effects can be captured by the model. We assume the corrected phenotype is drawn from a normal distribution centered around a complex, nonlinear function of the genes:

Assume:\\
\begin{equation}
Y_i \sim Normal(f(X_i),\sigma^2)
\end{equation}
where $f(X_i)$ captures how genes and gene pairs influence the corrected phenotype. We then break $f(X_i)$ into a sum of $k$ smaller functions where each of these functions captures the effects from a smaller subset of genes and gene pairs:
\begin{equation}
f(X_i) = \sum_{h=1}^k f^{(h)}(X_i)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
f^{(h)}(X_i) = \sum_{g_1 =1}^p \tilde{X}_{ig_1}\beta_{g_1}^{(h)} + \sum_{g_1 = 2}^p \sum_{g_2 \le g_1} \tilde{X}_{ig_1g_2}\beta_{g_1g_2}^{(h)}
\end{equation}
where the main effects are captured by $\sum_{g_1 =1}^p \tilde{X}_{ig_1}\beta_{g_1}^{(h)}$ and the pairwise interactions are captured by $\sum_{g_1 = 2}^p \sum_{g_2 \le g_1} \tilde{X}_{ig_1g_2}\beta_{g_1g_2}^{(h)}$.
\par
We use a sparsity-inducing spike and slab prior over each subset $S\subset \{1,2,\ldots,p\}$:

\begin{equation}
P(\beta_S^{(h)} \mid \mathbf{\eta}) = (1- \prod_{g \in s} \eta_{gh})\delta_0 + (\prod_{g\in s}\eta_{gh})\psi(\beta_S^{(h)})
\end{equation}
where the spike, $\delta_0$, is a point mass at zero
\begin{equation}
\delta_0 = P(\beta_S^{(h)} = 0)>0
\end{equation}
and the slab, $\psi(\beta_S^{(h)})$, is a multivariate normal
\begin{equation}
\psi(\beta_S^{(h)}) = \text{Normal}(\beta_S^{(h)} \mid \mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\sigma^2_{\beta}I)
\end{equation}
where $\sigma^2_{\beta}$ is estimated via Empirical Bayes.
\par
Let $\eta = \{\eta_{gh}\}$ be a binary matrix of inclusion indicators, where $\eta_{gh} = 1$ if gene $g$ is included in function $h$, and 0 otherwise. Define the active gene set for function $h$ as:
\begin{equation}
A_h = \{g : \eta_{gh}=1\}.
\end{equation}

Then, the prior on $\eta_{gh}$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
P(\eta_{gh}) = \tau_h^{\eta_{gh}}(1-\tau_h)^{1-\eta_{gh}} \cdot I\left(A_h \not\subseteq A_m \ \forall m \text{ or } A_h = \emptyset\right),
\end{equation}
where $\tau_h$ is the prior probability of a gene or gene-pair being included in function $h$. The inclusion probability follows a Beta prior:
\begin{equation}
\tau_h \sim \text{Beta}(M, \gamma),
\end{equation}
with $M$ and $\gamma$ fixed to control sparsity. We set $\gamma = p$, the total number of genes considered.
\par
This modeling framework allows us to prioritize genes and gene-pairs that are contributors to IA in DAISY children while naturally handling both non-linear effects and higher-order interactions. The use of natural splines introduces flexibility, while the spike-and-slab prior ensures sparsity and interpretability by selectively retaining only genes and interactions with substantial evidence.
\par
Importantly, this framework is hierarchical: we restrict the gene-level interaction model within each autosomomal chromosome (1-22). This two-stage approach melds the need for computational feasibility with a natural biological partition, enabling genome-scale modeling without exhaustively evaluating all gene-gene combinations.
\par
The resulting posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) serve as importance scores. This procedure offers a discovery-oriented path for understanding the polygenic, interactive architecture of IA risk in DAISY children, allowing for focused downstream analysis of variant-level contributions within prioritized genes and gene-gene interactions.

\subsection{Over-Representation Analysis}
To prioritize genes and gene-pairs identified by the Bayesian interaction model, we performed Over-Representation Analysis (ORA) using FUMA\cite{watanabeFunctionalMappingAnnotation2017}. ORA enables the identification of biological processes, pathways, and molecular functions that are statistically overrepresented among the high-priority genes, relative to a reference background. This approach offers biological interpretability by linking statistical evidence of interaction to known functional annotations. By mapping gene-level signals to higher-order biological functions, we aim to uncover pathways potentially enriched for nonlinear genetic interactions contributing to IA. Enrichment results were filtered at an FDR-adjusted $p$-value threshold of $0.1$, and top pathways were examined for relevance to immune regulation, pancreatic islet biology, and autoimmune disease mechanisms. The results of the ORA will provide an independent layer of biological validation for the statistical model outputs and guide the selection of genes and gene-pairs for downstream SNP-level epistasis visualization.

\subsection{Prioritized SNP Synergies}
\label{sec:gpair-syn}
To further investigate the fine-scale genetic architecture of gene-gene interactions, we construct a synergy matrix $\mathbf{S}$ for each gene-pair ${g_1, g_2}$ identified in Section~\ref{sec:bim} as interacting. Similar to Section~\ref{sec:withingene}, each matrix $\mathbf{S}$ encodes the pairwise synergy between SNPs $i$ and $j$, where $i \ne j$ and both $i$ and $j$ belong to the union of SNPs mapped to genes $g_1 \in \mathcal{G}_1$ and $g_2 \in \mathcal{G}_2$. That is, $i,j \in \mathcal{S}_1 \cup \mathcal{S}_2 = \mathcal{S}_{sub}$, where $\mathcal{S}_1, \mathcal{S}_2 \subset \mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{S}_1 \mapsto \mathcal{G}_1$, $\mathcal{S}_2 \mapsto \mathcal{G}_2$, with $\mathcal{G}_1, \mathcal{G}_2 \subset \mathcal{G}$.

Each entry $S_{ij}$ quantifies the pairwise epistatic interaction strength-captured as synergy-between SNPs $i$ and $j$ with respect to the disease phenotype. This enables the identification of specific variant combinations that may underlie the observed gene-level interaction signal. 
\par
To visualize and further explore these interactions, we construct a graph $G = (V, E)$ for each gene pair, where nodes $V$ represent SNPs and edge weights $E$ are determined by the corresponding synergy scores $S_{ij}$. A central challenge in this framework is deciding which edges to include in the graph: while statistical approaches such as permutation testing could be used to assess significance, such methods would require thousands of permutations per SNP pair and are therefore computationally intractable for discovery even within gene-pairs.
\par
As a pragmatic alternative, we define edge inclusion based on quantile thresholds of the synergy distribution. Specifically, we include edges whose synergy exceeds the $1-\alpha$ quantile, thereby prioritizing SNP pairs with the strongest positive epistatic signals. We set $\alpha = 0.01$ to focus on the top 1\% of interactions. While this approach does not explicitly model statistical significance, it enables interpretable and scalable exploration of variant-level interactions, and lays the foundation for future work incorporating rigorous uncertainty quantification.

\subsubsection{Logistic Regression}
\label{sec:logreg}
After the discovery analysis, we test for the direction of effect of the prioritized SNPs. It is not enough to know which SNPs may be interacting epistatically within gene-pairs determined to show evidence of epistasis, but we ideally would like to know how these SNPs interact in relation to disease risk. Are they protective or do they increase risk for developing IA? 
\par
In order to test the now targeted list of SNP-pairs from the result of the discovery approach taken in this paper, we use logistic regression. While this is not the best method given the assumption of interactions being potentially nonlinear, it is a common and interpretable approach. The functional model is:
\begin{equation}
\text{IA} \sim \text{SNP}_1 + \text{SNP}_2 + \text{SNP}_1\times \text{SNP}_2 + \text{Sex} + \mathbb{I}(\text{membership to high risk genotype DR3/4}) + \text{Ancestry PC1}
\end{equation}
This model will iterate over all pairs of SNPs determined to be important from the discovery analysis and will be corrected through a Benjamini-Hochberg correction at significance level $\alpha = 0.1$.

\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:res-over}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% 
We analyzed genetic data comprising 685,455 SNPs mapped to 20,859 genes across the 22 autosomal chromosomes in 359 DAISY individuals. All 20,859 genes were successfully summarized using diffusion kPCA, as described in Section~\ref{sec:dkpc}. These gene-level summaries were then used to model intra-chromosomal gene-gene interactions and gene main effects using a Bayesian semi-parametric approach outlined in Section~\ref{sec:bim}. Results highlight candidate genes and gene-pairs that may contribute to the onset of IA in DAISY children. 

\subsection{Within-Gene Epistasis and Gene Summaries}
\label{sec:res-within}

To quantify non-additive genetic effects within individual genes, we computed bivariate synergy scores for all pairwise combinations of SNPs mapped to the same gene. These scores measure the extent to which jointly knowing two SNPs reduces expected uncertainty about IA status beyond their individual effects. For each gene, the resulting synergy matrix was used to construct a Laplacian-esque Synergy matrix capturing SNP-level interaction structure, which was then summarized using diffusion kernel principal component analysis (kPCA), as described in Section~\ref{sec:dkpc}. The first diffusion PC was retained as a non-linear gene-level summary capturing within-gene epistasis.

\subsection{Bayesian Gene-Gene Interaction Modeling}
\label{sec:res-bim}

To identify gene-gene interactions contributing to IA, we modeled associations between gene-level summaries using a Bayesian nonlinear interaction model. This approach, described in Section~\ref{sec:bim}, employs spike-and-slab priors to jointly estimate main effects and pairwise interaction effects between genes. The input to the model consists of the first diffusion kernel principal component (kPCA) from within-gene synergy matrices, providing a non-linear summary of each gene’s internal epistatic structure.

\par
This model does not currently incorporate additional covariates such as sex, age, or population structure. Rather, it follows the process from \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022} to uncover interaction effects purely from genotype-derived gene summaries. Modeling was performed intra-chromosomally to reduce the computational burden -- considering all possible pairs of genes within each chromosome rather than across all chromosomes.

\par
Posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) were computed for each gene and gene-pair, quantifying the probability that a given effect is associated with IA. Genes and gene pairs were prioritized separately based on their PIPs. Figure~\ref{fig:PIP-dist} shows the distributions of PIPs for main and interaction effects. For downstream analysis, we retained genes and gene pairs with PIPs above the median of their respective distributions. This thresholding strategy differs slightly from \textit{Walakira et al.}, who retained all genes with non-zero PIPs. Given the larger number of genes analyzed in our study, this filtering step helps reduce noise while retaining more relevant candidates.
\par
Diagnostics for model convergence were saved and are discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. Notably, several Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains exhibited poor mixing, likely due to the small sample size. As a result, posterior estimates--particularly for interaction terms--should be interpreted with caution.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%% 
\subsubsection{Diffusion Parameter}
The diffusion kernel principal component analysis (kPCA) used to summarize within-gene epistasis depends on the choice of the diffusion parameter $\beta$, which governs the rate of information flow in the graph Laplacian. While we followed the strategy outlined in \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}, averaging across a grid of $\beta$ values from 0 to 10 in 0.1 increments, it is unclear whether this range is optimal for modeling epistatic interactions in IA or SNP interaction in general. More targeted methods for selecting $\beta$ based on cross-validation or biological prior information may improve signal resolution.

\subsubsection{Convergence}
MCMC diagnostics revealed that all chains exhibited poor mixing in the Bayesian interaction model. This lack of convergence is likely attributable to the relatively small sample size ($n = 359$) and the complexity of the model space, which includes both nonlinear main and interaction terms. Although posterior inclusion probabilities (PIPs) were still obtained and used for prioritization, estimates--particularly for interaction effects--should be interpreted with caution. Full traceplots for all chromosomes can be found in Section~\ref{sec:supp}.

\subsubsection{Assumption of Normality in the Response}
The current implementation of the Bayesian interaction model assumes a continuous, approximately normal response variable. While this is a reasonable approximation in some settings, with the response being binary status of disease, this assumption is not met. Following the procedure of \textit{Walakira et al.}\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}, who also had a binary response, we residualize the response with 7 ancestry PCs. This allowed the response to become continuous, yet still falling short of meeting the normality assumption. We instead have a bimodal distribution in the response. This mismatch introduces potential model misspecification, particularly in how effect sizes and inclusion probabilities are interpreted. A fully Bayesian logistic or probit model would be more appropriate for binary disease outcomes, but comes with substantial computational cost and additional complexity in posterior inference. Extending the model to explicitly accommodate discrete responses remains an important area for future methodological development.

\subsubsection{Power}
Despite leveraging a discovery-oriented Bayesian framework, this study remains underpowered to detect small to moderate epistatic effects, particularly in the context of complex gene-gene interactions. The sample size of 359 individuals limits the sensitivity of the hierarchical spike-and-slab prior used in the Bayesian interaction model, especially when modeling flexible, nonlinear relationships between gene-level summaries. While the model is designed to mitigate multiple testing through regularization, PIPs for gene pairs with modest effects may still be attenuated under limited sample size, potentially leading to false negatives or underestimated signals.

\par
Additionally, power constraints were more explicitly quantified for the SNP-level follow-up analysis. A power analysis using the \texttt{pwr}\cite{champelyPwrBasicFunctions2020} package in R indicated that, given the available sample size, only seven SNP-level interaction tests could be conducted at 80\% power. This limitation required strict prioritization of candidate SNP pairs, and may have contributed to the lack of statistically significant results after correction for multiple testing.

\par
The limited sample size presents a key constraint on both the detection and interpretation of epistatic effects. Future work will benefit from larger cohorts, which would increase statistical power, improve MCMC convergence, and support more robust modeling of subtle genetic interactions relevant to islet autoimmunity.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
This study presents a genome-wide, multi-stage framework for identifying epistatic interactions contributing to Islet Autoimmunity (IA) in DAISY children, leveraging both information-theory and Bayesian interaction modeling. Many Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) have been conducted in the context of Type 1 Diabetes (T1D) and through these analyses, much of the heritability has been characterized. Despite numerous GWAS, there is still a proportion of the genetic variance in IA and T1D that is missing\cite{pangMissingHeritabilityType2022}. Genome-Wide Association Interaction Studies (GWAIS) and other interactome (the entire set of interactions between DNA, RNA, proteins and metabolites within a cell\cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022}) analyses offer a potential solution to this problem. Despite limited power, our results highlight the promise of integrating SNP-level interaction structure with flexible gene-level modeling in Genome-Wide analysis, particularly in genetically complex diseases like IA. 
\par
GWAIS analyses tend to be more computationally intensive than their GWAS counterpart; this method is no different. We first used Synergy matrices to capture within-gene SNP interaction structure, summarizing each gene with a non-linear component from diffusion kernel principal component analysis. This approach allowed us to reduce dimensionality while summarizing SNP-level epistatic signals. The use of a High-Performance Computer (HPC) was necessary to conduct both within-gene epistasis summarization and between-gene epistasis detection. 
\par
Genes such as \textit{DCTN4} and \textit{CDH4}, which are involved in antigen presentation and cell adhesion, respectively, emerged as candidates with high internal synergy, supporting their potential role in IA risk. Although most interaction PIPs were modest--reflecting limited statistical power--top-ranked pairs such as \textit{PMM2/NOD2} and \textit{CDH4/PKIG} suggest novel, biologically plausible interactions. According to the UCSC Genome Browser:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{PMM2} (phosphomannomutase 2) is a key enzyme in glycosylation and is involved in the metobolic process (GO:0006013).
\item	\textit{NOD2} (nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 2) activates pro-inflammatory signaling through NF-$\kappa$B pathways, with known roles in Crohn’s disease and other inflammatory conditions and is involved with cytokine production in the immune response (GO:0002367).
\item \textit{CDH4} (cadherin 4, R-cadherin) is involved in cell adhesion (GO:0007155). 
\item \textit{PKIG} (protein kinase inhibitor gamma) is involved in the negative regulation of proteinkinase activity (GO:0006469).
\end{itemize}
These gene-pairs represent potential immune-metabolic crosstalk and suggest that this modeling approach can potentially uncover previously uncharacterized mechanisms of islet autoimmunity. Full gene annotations and genomic coordinates are available via the UCSC Genome Browser\cite{perezUCSCGenomeBrowser2025}.
\par
To interpret the biological relevance of these findings, we applied pathway enrichment using the FUMA\cite{watanabeFunctionalMappingAnnotation2017} platform. Genes with high PIPs were significantly downregulated in the pancreas and enriched in immune-related processes, including MHC class II antigen presentation and CD4\textsuperscript{+}/CD8\textsuperscript{+} T cell activation. These findings align with the known role of autoreactive T cells and impaired antigen processing in T1D pathogenesis. Additionally, trait enrichment in metabolite-associated genes supports prior evidence that early metabolic perturbations may influence IA development\cite{lamichhaneCirculatingMetabolicSignatures2023,maMetabolomicsAnalysisSerum2024}.
\par
We extended the framework proposed in \textit{Walakira et al.} \cite{walakiraDetectingGeneGene2022} by incorporating pathway enrichment to refine candidate gene selection for SNP-level interrogation. Using Reactome-informed filtering, we identified the top synergy SNP pair within each prioritized gene or gene-pair and performed logistic regression to assess directionality of effect. While no pairs survived FDR correction, the interaction between variants in \textit{PMM2} and \textit{NOD2} approached nominal significance (odds ratio = 5.08, 95\% CI: [0.94, 27.56]).
\par
Several limitations merit discussion. The diffusion parameter $\beta$ was fixed across genes, and MCMC chains exhibited poor mixing, likely due to the limited sample size. Our logistic regression power analysis indicated capacity for only seven tests at 80\% power, constraining downstream inference. Despite these challenges, the biologically relevant enrichment results provide reassurance that this approach captures meaningful signal at the gene-level.
\par
Future work should focus on increasing sample size, optimizing synergy parameterization, incorporating covariates into the Bayesian model, altering the response assumption of normality, and extending interaction modeling to cross-chromosome pairs. These steps will be essential to improving power, interpretability, and generalizability of epistasis detection strategies in complex disease genetics.
